Made simple. Made easy.
Made boring.

Round one read “boring” as uneventful and produced heart monitors. This round reads it the way you meant it — simple, easy, a non-issue — and every mark below is constructed on a 64-unit grid in code rather than generated, which is why the edges are true and the small sizes hold. Two ideas recur: subtraction (something removed) and the second meaning of “boring” — what a machinist does when drilling a precise hole.

What the marks worth copying actually do

Paul Rand · IBM

Eight stripes cut through three letters. Proof that a mark can be built from one repeated primitive and nothing else.

FedEx · negative space

The arrow nobody draws. The idea lives in the gap between two letters, so the mark rewards a second look without demanding one.

Chermayeff & Geismar · Chase

Four identical shapes, rotated. A pure ideograph — it depicts nothing and means something anyway.

Mies van der Rohe

“Less is more.” The dictum the whole modernist logo tradition runs on, and the literal product promise here.

Eight marks

monogram · negative space

Bored b

A lowercase b whose bowl is bored straight through.

The monogram and the pun in one shape. It reads as the letter b at a glance and as a machined part on a second look. Closest structural relative: the Beats b, but built on a grid instead of drawn.

My takeOnly mark that is a monogram, a pun and a machined object at once. Reads as “b” instantly, which no other option here does.

ideograph · negative space

Bore

A perfect circle bored clean through a solid square.

“Boring” is also what a machinist does: drilling a precise hole. The mark is a pun that only engineers get, sitting on top of the simplest possible subtraction — a square with something removed. It is the FedEx-arrow move: the meaning lives in the negative space.

My takeThe same idea without the letter. Cleaner, more universal, slightly less ownable.

Minus

One bar. The whole logo.

Not equals — minus. The operation we actually perform is subtraction: steps removed, tools removed, people removed from the loop. It is the least a logo can be while still being a logo, which is exactly the argument the company is making.

My takeThe most committed version of the joke. Also nearly invisible at 16px — look at the favicon test before you fall for it.
